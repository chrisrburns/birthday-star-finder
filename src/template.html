How visibility is decided. Each star's catalog position (epoch J2016.0) is moved by its proper motion to the observing date and precessed to that date. These precessed coordinates (mean equinox of the observing night) are what the table lists. Its altitude is then sampled at least every five minutes through the window. A star qualifies when, for the required time, it is both above your altitude limit and within your hour-angle limit (time from the meridian; leave it blank for no limit). "Max alt" and "Highest at" refer to that observable stretch. Refraction is ignored, which makes stars near the horizon appear up to about half a degree lower than they really are.

Twilight and the Moon. Sun and Moon positions use low-precision formulas good to about a minute of time for twilight. The window runs from evening to morning twilight on the night that begins on the chosen date, in the observatory's own time zone. Where the Sun never gets that low (high latitudes in summer), the next shallower twilight is used instead.

About this catalog. Very bright stars saturate Gaia's detectors, so stars have no Gaia source and come from other catalogs. Each star is labelled by its name from the file where there is one (Bayer or Flamsteed designation, such as α CMa or 35 Leo), otherwise by its HD, HIP or Gaia DR3 number, in that order. Magnitudes are V where available and Gaia G otherwise; colours are Gaia BP−RP where available and B−V otherwise, and the table marks which. SIMBAD links use the Gaia DR3 identifier, or the HIP or HD number for stars without one.
